## Bottom line

Rewritten in the repair pass. The conclusion (no evidence for a nine-figure institutional life-story business) survives, but it now rests on what vendors actually achieved, not on a price ceiling. The earlier "$8M-$28M, inside the $5-45M cap" claim is withdrawn.

1. No institutional life-story vendor has a sourced revenue of $10M. The largest figure found is LifeBio at "over $2 million in revenue with 48 employees" [sourced-secondary: https://practicalfounders.com/podcast/43-helping-seniors-tell-their-stories-and-fight-loneliness-with-easy-apps-beth-sanders/, 2023-05-05; page opened; host's summary of a founder interview, not a filing], about 23 years after the company says it was founded in 2000 [sourced: https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/health/program-areas/civil-monetary-penalty-program/project-proposals/LIfeBio_Application.pdf, 2020 application, repair pass]. That figure is 3.5 years old and it is [unknown] whether it includes grant income.
2. The largest identifiable payer in this category is public grant money, not operators. NIH RePORTER lists 7 National Institute on Aging small-business awards to LifeBio Inc totalling $5,371,563 for fiscal years 2020-2026 [sourced: https://api.reporter.nih.gov/v2/projects/search, organisation query "LIFEBIO", queried 2026-11-25], and CMS approved $305,739 of nursing-home penalty funds for two LifeBio projects in Tennessee [sourced: https://tn.gov/content/dam/tn/health/program-areas/cpm/LifeBio_CMSLetter.pdf, 2018-11-14, and https://www.tn.gov/content/dam/tn/health/program-areas/civil-monetary-penalty-program/cms-approval-letters/LifeBio_202004TN0427.pdf, 2020-07-13; sum estimated: $103,656.97 + $202,082.00]. The same source lists $1,761,423 to Storii, Inc. and $499,994 to MemoryWell LLC [sourced: same NIH RePORTER endpoint, queries "STORII" and "MEMORYWELL", 2026-11-25]. So LifeBio's commercial revenue from institutions may be well under $2M, and "mostly self-funded" (the podcast's phrase) no longer describes it.
3. Every other dedicated vendor shrank, pivoted or stayed charity-sized. Storii became an adult-day-care EHR (StoriiCare) and its life-story line is now a consumer phone product plus an NIA-funded study; MemoryWell's site resolves to a redirect stub [sourced: https://www.memorywell.com/, observed 2026-11-24 and 2026-11-25]; My Life Films reports £235.65k income [sourced: https://register-of-charities.charitycommission.gov.uk/en/charity-search/-/charity-details/5045434/financial-history, FY to 2025-02-28, repair pass].
4. The buyer exists and is counted: 41,465 assisted-living communities [sourced: https://www.ahcancal.org/Advocacy/State%20Data/US_AL_Fast_Facts_2026.pdf, June 2026], 14,742 nursing facilities [sourced: https://www.kff.org/medicaid/issue-brief/a-look-at-nursing-facility-characteristics/, July 2025 data], 6,706 hospices in 2024 [sourced: https://www.medpac.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Mar26_Ch10_MedPAC_Report_To_Congress_SEC.pdf, March 2026]. Federal rule names "life history", but the requirement is met by the assessment instrument, and platform vendors already ship capture as a feature: LifeLoop ("over 4,700 senior living communities", a vendor self-claim) has "Life Story" [sourced: https://lifeloop.com/, read 2026-11-25; https://lifeloop.com/our-solution/features/life-story, read 2026-11-24], and TSOLife raised a $43 million Series B for a platform that transcribes resident interviews [sourced: https://skillednursingnews.com/2025/05/provider-of-ai-dashboard-for-nursing-home-resident-engagement-and-care-outcomes-tsolife-completes-43m-series-b-funding/, 2025-05-07, repair pass].
5. Price evidence is thin and mostly stale. Every 2017 institutional price (LifeBio about $2,000 a year for fewer than 50 people and $5,000-$10,000 for a very large campus; Storii $2,500 a year for 1-25 residents and $4,000 for 26-50; MemoryWell $450 per story) comes from one trade article [sourced: https://www.iadvanceseniorcare.com/story-of-my-life/, 2017-01-14]. The repair pass found one current life-story price: unlisted LifeBio shop pages showing group licences at $149.00 for an annual census of 100-199 people and $349.00 for 300-399, both labelled "Monthly Billing" [sourced: https://lifebio.azurewebsites.net/lifebio-licensing-annual-census-or-headcount-of-50-99-people-monthly-billing-copy and https://lifebio.azurewebsites.net/lifebio-licensing-annual-census-or-headcount-of-200-299-people-monthly-billing-copy, read 2026-11-25; whether the amount is per month is not stated on the page]. Read as monthly, that is about $9-$18 per person per year [estimated: $149 x 12 / 199 to $149 x 12 / 100], lower than the 2017 floor of $40. Current prices for LifeLoop Life Story, TSOLife and Storii's hospice / agency programmes are [unknown].
6. The ceiling arithmetic does not cap the variant on its own. At LifeLoop-level penetration (at most 8.4% of facilities), the file's own prices give anything from about $1.7M to about $85M a year, and per-community prices give $8M-$49M [estimated: section 2, Q8]. Reaching $100M at that penetration needs about $530 per resident per year, which is 1.2 times MemoryWell's one-off $450 story fee and 30 to 59 times LifeBio's current list licence [estimated: Q8]. What argues against the high end is behaviour, not arithmetic: the $450-per-story model was abandoned by its vendor, the roughly $9,000-$10,000-per-home full-service model was paid by penalty-fund grants, and the surviving self-serve price sits at the bottom of the range.
7. The only adjacent business above $10M is Famileo, "près de 14 millions d'euros" revenue in 2024 by its founders' account [sourced-secondary: https://www.bretagne-economique.com/portraits/famileo-260-000-familles-abonnees-et-des-horizons-europeens/, 2025-12-12; page opened; founder claims in regional press, no statutory accounts checked]. It is a family-news gazette launched in 2015 [sourced: https://www.famileopro.com/fr-fr/tarifs, read 2026-11-25], so it took about nine years to reach that figure [estimated: 2024 - 2015]. The care home pays a small fee, families use it free inside partner homes, and the family photo flow is the product. It is evidence for family-photo engagement in care homes, not for life-story capture.

Q1 market structure and counts. Q2 what operators already buy for engagement and what they pay. Q3 life-story vendors in institutions and their scale. Q4 regulatory and quality drivers and the evidence base. Q5 sales reality. Q6 family as payer inside the institution. Q7 insurer / employer / pre-need channel. ## 2. Findings

### Q1. Market structure

- Assisted living (US): 41,465 communities, 1,369,317 licensed units, 1,016,424 residents, 33 units on average; size mix 43% small (4-10 units), 14% medium (11-25), 33% large (26-100), 10% over 100; 44% of residents have Alzheimer's or other dementia; average yearly cost $74,400; 512,300 employees [sourced: https://www.ahcancal.org/Advocacy/State%20Data/US_AL_Fast_Facts_2026.pdf, June 2026]. 57.3% chain-affiliated, 42.7% independent [sourced: https://www.ahcancal.org/Assisted-Living/Facts-and-Figures/Pages/default.aspx, undated page, read 2026-11-24].

### Q4. Regulatory and quality drivers

- Nursing homes: the comprehensive assessment must cover "a resident's needs, strengths, goals, life history and preferences", including "customary routine", "psychosocial well-being" and "activity pursuit" [sourced: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/483.20, 42 CFR 483.20(b)(1), read 2026-11-24]. 42 CFR 483.21 (care planning) does not contain the words "history" or "life story"; it requires person-centred plans and services that are "culturally-competent and trauma-informed" [sourced: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/483.21, read 2026-11-24]. Activities must be "based on the comprehensive assessment and care plan and the preferences of each resident" [sourced: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/483.24, 483.24(c), read 2026-11-24].
- Survey tags, now verified in the CMS State Operations Manual, Appendix PP (Rev. 225, issued 2024-08-08): F636 is the comprehensive-assessment tag that carries the "life history" wording, F656 is "Develop/Implement Comprehensive Care Plan", and F679 is the activities tag [sourced: https://www.cms.gov/medicare/provider-enrollment-and-certification/guidanceforlawsandregulations/downloads/appendix-pp-state-operations-manual.pdf, read 2026-11-25]. A text search of the extracted guidance (about 2.4 MB) finds the phrase "life history" once, in the quoted regulation, and does not find "life story"; occurrences split across a line break could be missed. The activities guidance says activities for residents with dementia "must be individualized and customized based on the resident's previous lifestyle (occupation, family, hobbies), preferences and comforts" and that the facility "may use, but need not duplicate, information from other sources, such as the RAI/MDS assessment ... and resident and family interviews" [sourced: same document, F679 guidance, read 2026-11-25].
- So the requirement is satisfied by the MDS/RAI instrument and an intake interview. No rule or surveyor guidance requires a narrative, a book, photos or a third-party tool. A regulatory mandate therefore supports a free form or an EHR field, not a paid programme. One vendor has still used the quality regime as a funding route: LifeBio's Tennessee applications tie the programme to quality measures and F-tag reporting and were paid from penalty funds (Q3).
- Memory care: no national requirements [sourced-secondary: search extract of a Scripps Gerontology Center 2019 report; page not opened]. Iowa requires "a complete assessment of the resident applicant's physical, mental, social and" behavioural status before admission to a dementia unit [sourced: https://www.legis.iowa.gov/docs/iac/rule/06-11-2014.481.57.7.pdf, 2014 version, read 2026-11-24]. New York and Indiana ask for social or pre-dementia personal history in guidance or forms [sourced-secondary: search extracts; pages not opened]. NCAL publishes an annual "Assisted Living State Regulatory Review" covering every state [sourced: https://www.ahcancal.org/Assisted-Living/Policy/Pages/state-regulations.aspx, read 2026-11-25]; it was located but not read state by state, so a 50-state count of social-history rules is [unknown].
- Hospice: counselling must include an organised bereavement programme, available "up to 1 year following the death of the patient" [sourced: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/418.64, 418.64(d)(1), read 2026-11-24]. The brief says 13 months; the regulation says one year. Thirteen months is a programme practice at some hospices (for example Mayo Clinic Hospice, "up to 13 months after your loved one's death") [sourced-secondary: search extract of https://www.mayoclinic.org/departments-centers/hospice/sections/bereavement/gnc-20481748; HTTP 403 on fetch 2026-11-25]. Medicare does not pay for it: "Statute requires that hospices offer bereavement services to family members of deceased Medicare patients ... however, the statute prohibits Medicare payment for these services"; bereavement and volunteer costs sit in non-reimbursable cost centres, and including them "would reduce the aggregate Medicare margin for 2023 by at most 1.1 percentage points and 0.3 percentage points, respectively" [sourced: https://www.medpac.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Mar26_Ch10_MedPAC_Report_To_Congress_SEC.pdf, endnote 25, March 2026, repair pass]. Legacy projects are not named in 418.64 and no CMS source was found that makes them separately payable; that they are funded from the per diem, volunteers or philanthropy is an inference from the absence, not a sourced statement [unknown: no CMS document on legacy work was found or searched for beyond the regulation and MedPAC].
- Size of the hospice bereavement cost pool: at most about $283M in 2023, or about $219 per Medicare hospice decedent; scaled to 2024 spending, at most about $311M, or about $234 per decedent [estimated: 1.1% x $25.7B / 1.29M decedents; 1.1% x $28.3B / 1.33M decedents; all inputs from MedPAC March 2026; treats margin points as a share of Medicare hospice payments and applies a 2023 ratio to 2024]. The earlier $334M and $257 figures are withdrawn. Most of the pool is counsellor salary. A legacy product competes for a slice of it.
- Evidence base, reminiscence therapy for dementia (Cochrane, 22 studies, 1,972 people): no important effect on quality of life overall (SMD 0.11, 95% CI -0.12 to 0.33); probable slight benefit in care homes (SMD 0.46, 0.18 to 0.75; 3 studies, 193 participants); cognition SMD 0.11 "of doubtful clinical importance"; MMSE +1.87 points; communication SMD -0.51 (negative is better) [sourced: Woods et al. 2018, PMID 29493789, https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29493789&rettype=abstract&retmode=text, read 2026-11-24].
- Dignity therapy. Largest single RCT (441 randomised, 326 analysed): "No significant differences were noted in the distress levels" on primary outcomes; patients reported it more helpful and better for family on secondary self-report (p<0.0001) [sourced: Chochinov et al. 2011, PMID 21741309, https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=21741309&rettype=abstract&retmode=text, read 2026-11-24]. Meta-analyses added in the repair pass: 16 studies, 1,202 palliative-care participants: dignity-related distress MD -3.54 (95% CI -4.66 to -2.42), hope MD 2.27 (1.32 to 3.22), quality of life SMD 0.45 (CI printed as 0.15 to 1.14), no significant difference in depression (MD -0.44, -1.09 to 0.20) or anxiety (MD -0.21, -1.68 to 1.27); most studies rated medium quality [sourced: Wulandari and Rochmawati 2024, PMID 37980718, https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=37980718,35635908&rettype=abstract&retmode=text, read 2026-11-25]. 14 RCTs in cancer patients: hope MD 3.41 (2.82 to 4.00), anxiety SMD -0.75 (-1.32 to -0.18), depression SMD -1.01 (-1.60 to -0.43) at post-intervention [sourced: Zhang, Li and Hu 2022, PMID 35635908, same URL, read 2026-11-25]. The two reviews disagree on depression and anxiety.
- Reading: the evidence supports "residents and families like it", a small care-home quality-of-life effect for reminiscence, and patient-reported benefits on hope and dignity-related distress for dignity therapy. None of these abstracts reports cost or utilisation outcomes, so the evidence does not support a cost-saving claim that would unlock payer reimbursement.
