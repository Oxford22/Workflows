## 2. Findings

### Q1. Illinois BIPA (740 ILCS 14)

**Whose consent.** Section 15(b): no private entity may "collect, capture, purchase, receive through trade, or otherwise obtain a person's or a customer's biometric identifier or biometric information, unless it first: (1) informs the subject or the subject's legally authorized representative in writing ... (2) informs the subject ... of the specific purpose and length of term ... and (3) receives a written release executed by the subject of the biometric identifier" [sourced: https://codes.findlaw.com/il/chapter-740-civil-liabilities/il-st-sect-740-14-15/, text current as of 2025-01-01; ilga.gov returned 503 on four attempts]. The consent must come from the **subject**, not the uploader. A "written release" is "informed written consent, electronic signature, or, in the context of employment, a release executed by an employee" [sourced: https://codes.findlaw.com/il/chapter-740-civil-liabilities/il-st-sect-740-14-10/]. Section 15(a) also requires a public written retention/destruction policy (destroy when purpose satisfied or within 3 years of last interaction [sourced: same 14/15 page]) and 15(c) bars selling or otherwise profiting from the data [sourced: same 14/15 page].

**Does a face template from a photo count?** "Biometric identifier" means "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry"; "Biometric identifiers do not include ... photographs" [sourced: FindLaw 14/10]. The photograph exclusion has not saved photo apps: Rivera v. Google (face grouping), the Facebook tag-suggestions MDL, Shutterfly and Apple Photos all survived dismissal on the theory that the face-geometry scan derived from the photo is the identifier [sourced: outcomes below]. Falsification attempt: no court found that face grouping from uploaded photos is outside BIPA; the only successful defences were extraterritoriality (Amazon/Microsoft, Diversity-in-Faces dataset) [sourced: https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/amazon-and-microsoft-win-summary-judgment-illinois-bipa-lawsuits-based, secondary] and the on-device "control" defence below.

**Damages and the 2024 amendment.** Section 20: liquidated damages of $1,000 (negligent) or $5,000 (intentional/reckless) per violation plus fees [sourced: https://codes.findlaw.com/il/chapter-740-civil-liabilities/il-st-sect-740-14-20/]. New 20(b)/(c) (SB 2979, Public Act 103-0769, signed 2024-08-02 [sourced: https://blogs.duanemorris.com/classactiondefense/2024/08/05/illinois-corrects-the-bipas-cataclysmic-jobs-killing-damages-regime-in-new-reform-legislation/, 2024-08-05, secondary]): an entity that "in more than one instance, collects, captures ..." the same person's biometrics "has committed a single violation of subsection (b) of Section 15 for which the aggrieved person is entitled to, at most, one recovery" [sourced: FindLaw 14/20]. Clay v. Union Pacific, No. 25-2185 (7th Cir. 2026-04-01) held the amendment applies retroactively to pending cases; Sidley describes the effect as eliminating per-scan damages "in federal cases within the Seventh Circuit" and notes that "several trial courts" had previously held the amendment prospective only; Sidley also recalls that in Cothron v. White Castle (2023 IL 128004) the Illinois Supreme Court "put that issue to the legislature to resolve" [sourced: https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/04/08/seventh-circuit-limits-potential-damages-under-bipa-holds-2024-amendment-applies-retroactively/, 2026-04-08]. Whether Illinois state courts or the Illinois Supreme Court have since ruled on retroactivity: [unknown — Sidley does not address it; no search was possible this round]. Net: in federal court exposure is now per-person, not per-scan; duties are unchanged. No actual harm is needed to sue (Rosenbach v. Six Flags, 2019) [sourced: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biometric_Information_Privacy_Act, secondary]. At least 100 BIPA class actions were filed in 2025 [sourced: https://www.privacyworld.blog/2025/12/2025-year-in-review-biometric-privacy-litigation/, 2025-12-11].

**Does on-device processing change exposure? Yes, materially, but only if the company never touches the template.** G.T. v. Samsung Electronics America (7th Cir., Aug 2026): Samsung's preinstalled Gallery app created face templates on-device and clustered photos; the court held that "possessing, collecting, capturing, and obtaining biometric data under BIPA all require the defendant to have some degree of control over that data," assumed without deciding that the templates are biometric identifiers, and affirmed dismissal because plaintiffs never alleged the data left the device or that Samsung could access it [sourced: https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/seventh-circuit-holds-that-bipa-does-not-reach-biometric-data-that-remains-on-a-users-device, 2026-08-20; https://www.wlf.org/2026/09/01/wlf-legal-pulse/possess-or-collect-under-illinois-bipa-seventh-circuit-resolves-trial-court-split/, 2026-09-01 (links the opinion, dated 2026-08-07)]. Mayer Brown's takeaways: create and store biometrics only on-device, never send to company or third-party servers, let users disable and delete, keep disclosures anyway; the decision binds federal courts in the Seventh Circuit, not Illinois state courts; verbatim: "Where the allegations or underlying facts establish that biometric data is transmitted to company or third-party servers, or where the defendant retains access to it, defendants will likely need a different defense strategy" [sourced: Mayer Brown 2026-08-20, re-fetched and quote verified 2026-11-03]. The Apple litigation is the live test of that boundary: Apple itself argued the Samsung ruling should end the case [sourced: idtechwire 2026-08-27] and lost on the encryption argument [sourced: Law360 summary 2026-10-09]; whether the iCloud-sync subclass is the one that survived is [unknown] (above). For our product — whose premise is merging relatives' libraries — templates cross devices, so the Samsung defence is weak unless sync is end-to-end encrypted and the company provably cannot read templates (the Ente pattern, Q6); and the Apple ruling shows a court was unwilling to treat encryption alone as dispositive at summary judgment.

**Geofencing in practice.** Google's face-grouping help page does not name any state; it says "This feature isn't available in all geographic regions, all domains, or all account types," "These models may be considered biometric data in some jurisdictions," and "When 'Face Groups' is on, you let us know that you want models of the faces that appear in your photos to be made" — i.e., Google takes consent from the account holder only, and never mentions the people in the photos [sourced: https://support.google.com/photos/answer/6128838, re-verified 2026-11-03: no "Illinois" or "Texas" on the page]. Turning it off deletes face groups, face models and labels; models are "stored and used until you either delete them ... or your Google Photos account is inactive for more than two years" [sourced: same]. Google's Ask Photos help page says only "Currently, Ask Photos is rolling out to a select number of countries and regions" and "To use Ask Photos, you need to confirm your 'me' face group"; no state is named [sourced: https://support.google.com/photos/answer/15318661]. ### Q2. Texas CUBI (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code §503.001)

Text: biometric identifier = "retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or record of hand or face geometry"; a person may not capture one for a commercial purpose unless it "(1) informs the individual before capturing the biometric identifier; and (2) receives the individual's consent"; must "destroy the biometric identifier within a reasonable time, but not later than the first anniversary" of the purpose expiring; "civil penalty of not more than $25,000 for each violation"; attorney general enforces (no private right) [sourced: https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._and_com._code_section_503.001, page verified 2025-05-26]. Again the consent is the *individual's* (the face), not the uploader's. TRAIGA (HB 149, effective 2026-01-01) amends CUBI: an individual "has not provided consent ... based solely on the existence of the image or other publicly available source," and exempts biometrics used to train/develop AI "unless the system is used or deployed to uniquely identify a specific individual" [sourced: https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/texas-enacts-ai-consumer-protection-law/ and https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/alerts-practices-dpc-with-traiga-lone-star-state-leans, July 2025, secondary; the public.law statute page did not yet show the amendment]. Our index exists precisely to identify specific individuals, so the AI-training exemption does not help.

### Q3. Other states and federal

- **Washington RCW 19.375 (enacted 2017: session-law note "[2017 c 299 s 3.]" [sourced: https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.375.010]):** "biometric identifier" excludes "a physical or digital photograph, video or audio recording or data generated therefrom"; "enroll" = capture, convert to a reference template, store in a database matching to an individual; enrolling for a commercial purpose requires "notice, obtaining consent, or providing a mechanism to prevent the subsequent use"; "This chapter may be enforced solely by the attorney general" [sourced: https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.375.010, .020, .030]. Templates derived from photographs are arguably excluded; a commentator notes the drafting is ambiguous on whether "data generated therefrom" reaches photographs [sourced: https://news.bloomberglaw.com/privacy-and-data-security/washingtons-new-biometric-privacy-statute-and-how-it-compares-to-illinois-and-texas-law, secondary].
- **Washington My Health My Data Act, RCW 19.373 (enacted 2023: session-law note "[2023 c 191 s 3]" [sourced: https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010]):** "biometric data" includes "Imagery of the iris, retina, fingerprint, face, hand, palm, vein patterns, and voice recordings" from which an identifier template can be extracted; biometric data is listed as "consumer health data"; "consent" is "a clear affirmative act that signifies a consumer's freely given, specific, informed" agreement and acceptance of general terms does not count; a violation "is an unfair or deceptive act ... for the purpose of applying the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW" (which carries a private right of action) [sourced: https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010 and .090]. - **Oregon ORS 646A.570:** biometric data excludes photographs and "facial mapping or facial geometry, unless ... generated for the purpose of identifying a specific consumer or was used to identify a specific consumer"; biometric data is "sensitive data" (opt-in consent, "an affirmative act") [sourced: https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_646a.570]. ### Q4. EU

- **GDPR.** Art. 9(1) prohibits processing "biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" unless an Art. 9(2) ground applies; 9(2)(a) is "explicit consent"; 9(2)(e) "manifestly made public by the data subject" [sourced: https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/]. Recital 51: "The processing of photographs should not systematically be considered to be processing of special categories" — they become biometric only "when processed through a specific technical means allowing the unique identification" [sourced: https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-51/]. Face embeddings used to identify relatives are squarely Art. 9; the lawful basis is effectively explicit consent *of each data subject*. Fines for Art. 9 infringements fall in the upper tier, Art. 83(5)(a): "up to 20 000 000 EUR, or in the case of an undertaking, up to 4 % of the total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher" [sourced: https://gdpr-info.eu/art-83-gdpr/].
- **Household exemption and its limit for a company.** Art. 2(2)(c) excludes processing "by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity" [sourced: https://gdpr-info.eu/art-2-gdpr/]. EDPB Statement 01/2026 on smart glasses (adopted 2026-10-13): the exemption is narrow (sharing with one family member may qualify; public posting does not) and "the GDPR still applies to device manufacturers and providers of linked applications or services" even where the user is household-exempt [sourced: https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/edpb-releases-smart-glasses-statement, 2026-10-28]. Dutch AP facial-recognition guidance (May 2024, as summarised by A&O Shearman): on-device biometric authentication can be household-exempt only if five conditions hold, including that "The user's biometric data is stored in a secure way and cannot be accessed by third parties (for instance, they are not stored in an external database)" and "The biometric data is stored on the device itself using state of the art encryption"; the other three are private use for device access, no imposition by an employer or third party with a free alternative, and (for access control) the system "only indicates whether the detection has been successful or not" [sourced: https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/netherlands-the-dutch-data-protection-authority-publishes-guidance-on-facial-recognition-may-2-2024, 2024-06-10, quotes re-verified 2026-11-03; the AP's own page returned 403]. Those criteria are written for device unlock, not photo libraries; a family index that names people fails criterion five by design. EDPB Guidelines 3/2019 (video devices), §5.1: "The use of video surveillance including biometric recognition functionality installed by private entities for their own purposes ... will, in most cases, require explicit consent from all data subjects (Article 9 (2) (a))" [sourced: https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_201903_video_devices_en_0.pdf, PDF text; the context is video surveillance, not consumer photo services, so it is analogy only]. So the user is exempt; the company running the matching is not.

- **EU AI Act, Art. 5.** Art. 5(1)(e) bans AI "that create or expand facial recognition databases through the untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage"; 5(1)(g) bans biometric categorisation inferring "race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs"; both apply from 2025-02-02 [sourced: https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/]. The Commission's prohibited-practices guidelines (C(2025) 5052 final, Brussels 29.7.2025, approved 2025-02-04; "non-binding, with authoritative interpretations reserved for the Court of Justice") set four cumulative conditions for 5(1)(e): "(i) ... 'placing on the market', 'the putting into service for this specific purpose' or the 'use' of an AI system; (ii) for the purpose of creating or expanding facial recognition databases; (iii) the means to populate the database are through AI tools for untargeted scraping; and (iv) the sources of the images are either from the internet or CCTV footage" (para 224); "'Scraping' typically refers to using web crawlers, bots, or other means to extract data or content from different sources, including CCTV, websites or social media, automatically" (para 227); "'Untargeted' relates to a technique that operates like a 'vacuum cleaner', absorbing as much data and information as possible ... without a specific focus on a given individual or group of individuals" (para 228); "the fact that a person has published facial images of themselves on a social media platform does not mean that that person has given his or her consent for those images to be included in a facial recognition database" (para 232); a facial recognition database "may be temporary, centralised or decentralised" and need not have recognition as its sole purpose (para 226); out of scope: "Facial image databases that are not used for the recognition of persons" (para 234) [sourced: https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/112367, PDF text extracted locally; status and date from https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-prohibited-artificial-intelligence-ai-practices-defined-ai-act, published 2025-02-04, updated 2025-07-31]. The guidelines say nothing about private, user-uploaded libraries. My inference, not a sourced finding: a family index built from photos the user and relatives upload fails conditions (iii) and (iv) — no crawler/bot and no internet/CCTV source — so 5(1)(e) does not reach it; but importing a relative's social-media export is still "internet"-sourced imagery for which posting is not consent (para 232), and any web crawl for a relative's face would meet (iii)/(iv) unless clearly "targeted" (para 229) [estimated: inference from paras 224, 227–229, 232; no guidance or case on consumer libraries found]. The FPF explainer (2026-03-17) summarises the same points and likewise does not address private libraries [sourced: https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-the-eu-ai-act-understanding-the-ban-of-the-untargeted-scraping-of-facial-images-and-facial-recognition-databases/, 2026-03-17].
- **EU AI Act, Annex III.** Annex III 1(a) makes "remote biometric identification systems" high-risk, excluding verification "to confirm that a specific natural person is the person he or she claims to be" [sourced: https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/]. Art. 3(41): an RBI system is "an AI system for the purpose of identifying natural persons, without their active involvement, typically at a distance through the comparison of a person's biometric data with the biometric data contained in a reference database"; Art. 3(35) "biometric identification" = "the automated recognition of physical, physiological, behavioural, or psychological human features for the purpose of establishing the identity of a natural person by comparing biometric data of that individual to biometric data of individuals stored in a database"; Art. 3(36) verification is "one-to-one" [sourced: https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/3/]. The Commission guidelines gloss the definition as covering "only the identification functionality," implying "the absence of active involvement of the persons concerned (i.e. no active participation)" and capture "typically at a distance," compared against "a reference database (such as a repository ...)" (paras 298–299) [sourced: C(2025) 5052 PDF, extracted locally]. Read literally, a family index does identify people "without their active involvement" (the aunt did nothing), from images captured at a distance, against a reference database of named clusters — every element is present on the text. Whether regulators would treat a consumer photo organiser as an RBI "placed on the market" for that purpose, or whether an Art. 6(3) derogation applies, is unresolved: no guidance, decision or commentary on consumer photo apps was found [unknown; the elements analysis is my inference, estimated from the Art. 3 text]. ### Q5. FTC

- **Everalbum order (issued 2021-05-06).** Definitions: "Biometric Information" = data that "depicts or describes the physical or biological traits of an identified or identifiable person, including depictions (including images)"; "Face Embedding" = "data, such as a numeric vector, derived in whole or in part from an image of an individual's face"; "Affected Work Product" = "any models or algorithms developed in whole or in part using Biometric Information" from Ever users. Section II: before using a User's Biometric Information to "(1) create a Face Embedding or (2) train, develop, or alter any face recognition model," Respondent must "Clearly and Conspicuously disclose to the User ... separate and apart from any 'privacy policy,' 'terms of use' page" all purposes, and "Obtain the affirmative express consent of the User from whom Respondent collected the Biometric Information"; the provision does not apply to products "only offered to Users outside the United States." Section III: within 30 days delete photos of users who deactivated; within 90 days delete all Face Embeddings of users who have not consented; within 90 days delete any Affected Work Product, each with a sworn statement. ### Q7. Consent architecture and its feature cost

Practitioner guidance converges. The one 2026 alert that is specifically about a consumer photo app is Mayer Brown's on G.T. v. Samsung (the Samsung Gallery face-grouping feature): design for on-device, let users disable and delete, keep written disclosures and retention policy anyway, and expect "a different defense strategy" if data reaches company servers [sourced: Mayer Brown 2026-08-20]; WLF's note on the same case [sourced: WLF 2026-09-01]. Generic biometric alerts: inventory every face-touching feature, map whether it extracts geometry, get written release, publish retention policy, fix vendor contracts [sourced: https://www.shumaker.com/insight/when-ai-generated-images-trigger-biometric-privacy-risks-new-lawsuits-highlight-emerging-compliance-challenges-for-businesses/, 2026-10-29 — its subject is AI-generated images, not photo apps]; "prior opt-in consent" for sensitive data [sourced: Linklaters 2025-05-19]; Venable: assess per use case, retention policy, never sell [sourced: Venable July 2026].

Architecture that fits every regime above:
1. **Embeddings computed on-device; company holds only ciphertext.** Satisfies G.T. v. Samsung "control" test, Dutch AP household criteria (as far as they transfer), and removes the company as GDPR controller of templates. Cost: no server-side cross-library clustering; web client loses faces (Ente's limitation); cross-device merge needs E2EE sync keyed by the user. Caveat: the Apple court rejected encryption as a liability shield at summary judgment [sourced: Law360 summary 2026-10-09], so ciphertext-only is necessary, not sufficient.
2. **Anonymous clusters are fine; named identities need the subject.** A cluster becomes a "person" only when (a) the subject accepts an invite and ticks a separate, unavoidable biometric-consent screen (BIPA 15(b) written release = electronic signature; Everalbum-style disclosure; GDPR 9(2)(a)), (b) a parent/guardian does so for a minor, or (c) the person is marked deceased (MyHeritage pattern; BIPA/CUBI protect living individuals). Cost: the non-consenting aunt stays "Unknown 7" — the cross-generation index is only as complete as the consent graph. [estimated: inference from statute text; no case on anonymous on-device clusters found]
3. **No template ever leaves one family vault; no training on user faces.** Blocks the Everalbum/OkCupid/Clearview fact pattern and TRAIGA's identification carve-out; 15(c) no-profit.
4. **Retention:** delete templates on withdrawal; on account inactivity no later than 3 years after last interaction in Illinois [sourced: FindLaw 740 ILCS 14/15(a)], 24 months in Colorado [sourced: Thompson Hine 2024; Hunton 2024-06-27] (MyHeritage uses 3 months after last use [sourced: MyHeritage privacy policy]; Google uses 2 years' inactivity [sourced: support.google.com/photos/answer/6128838]); and never later than purpose end — Texas requires destruction "not later than the first anniversary" of the purpose expiring [sourced: texas.public.law §503.001]. Publish the policy.
5. **Geofence:** disable server-side matching for IL and TX accounts (Google, Amazon, Adobe all do) unless architecture 1 is in place; treat WA as IL-grade because of MHMDA's private right of action; EU/UK = explicit opt-in + on-device only. Cost: features vanish for 12,812,508 Illinois + 29,145,505 Texas residents = 41,958,013 people, 12.7% of the US 2020 population of 331,449,281 [sourced: 2020 Census, "Table 2. Resident Population for the 50 States, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico: 2020 Census", https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/data/apportionment/apportionment-2020-table02.pdf, PDF text extracted locally; US total from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census, secondary; 41,958,013 / 331,449,281 = 12.66%] — i.e. the markets the incumbents have vacated.
6. **Keep the FTC-facing promises literal:** separate consent screen, no "may collect" vagueness, sworn-deletion-grade logs.

**Feature cost of per-subject consent, quantified as far as honesty allows.** The share of a family corpus that stays un-named under rule 2 is: un-named share of face appearances = Σ over living non-consenting people of their appearance share. Inputs needed: (a) number of distinct living people in a corpus, (b) their appearance-frequency distribution, (c) the invite-acceptance rate. No public data on (a)–(c) for family libraries was found, so the actual value is [unknown]. A reproducible worked scenario, with inputs that are assumptions and not measurements: if 10 living people account for 90% of face appearances and 10 minor relatives for the rest, a 60% acceptance rate among the 10 (6 of 10 consent; assume equal appearance shares of 9% each) leaves 4 × 9% + 10% = 46% of appearances un-named; at 90% acceptance it is 1 × 9% + 10% = 19% [estimated: arithmetic on the stated assumed inputs only; replace with measured inputs before use]. Two sourced anchors bound the problem: the Rivera class was defined as people who *appeared in* photos, not account holders [sourced: Fox32 2022-09-12], so subjects ≠ users is the litigated reality; and MyHeritage, the one product that engineered around this, auto-names 0% of living people [sourced: MyHeritage privacy policy].

Residual: even with 1–6, a plaintiff can allege the company "obtains" templates via sync (the Apple iCloud-subclass theory, whose survival is [unknown]), and the Illinois Supreme Court could reject Clay/Samsung reasoning ([unknown] whether it has been asked). The honest statement is that the product's core promise (index relatives who never signed up) cannot be delivered for living non-consenting people in IL/TX/EU without accepting BIPA-class exposure.
